Rental kitchens have a specific kind of ugly. Not bad exactly, just neutral to the point of
saying nothing. Builder-grade cabinets, a light fixture that came with the building in 1998, and
counters you're not allowed to touch. You can still fix it. You just have to work around the
lease, not against it.

I've lived in five rentals. Every one had a kitchen I wasn't allowed to paint, drill, or
permanently alter in any way. Here's what actually made a difference, and what I'd skip.

## Peel-and-stick backsplash, but only the good kind

Cheap peel-and-stick tile looks cheap within a month, curling at the edges near the stove. Spend
the extra fifteen dollars on a vinyl or gel-based product rated for kitchens, and it can genuinely
last through a lease and come off clean at the end.

Install it dry, no water, following the seams carefully, and skip anywhere it'll take direct heat
from a range hood. That part still needs to stay bare.

## Cabinet hardware is the highest return upgrade in this list

Swapping builder-grade knobs for something with actual weight to it changes a kitchen faster than
almost anything else on this list, and it takes an hour. Keep every original knob in a labeled
bag. Screw them back in before move-out and no one will ever know you changed a thing.

Brushed brass or matte black both read as intentional. What doesn't work is mixing three finishes
across one kitchen because you bought whatever was cheapest at three different times.

## Under-cabinet lighting with no wiring involved

Battery-powered or plug-in LED strips solve the single worst thing about most rental kitchens,
which is a single overhead fixture doing all the work. Stick-on LED strips under the upper
cabinets take twenty minutes and use adhesive that comes off clean.

It's a small fix. It also might be the one that makes the biggest visible difference for the
least effort, which is exactly the kind of trade I'll take every time.

## A tension rod under the sink

This one isn't glamorous, but a tension rod installed under the sink, with S-hooks holding a
spray bottle and dish gloves, frees up an entire cabinet shelf you were losing to clutter. It
costs about six dollars and involves zero tools.

## Open shelving, without removing a single cabinet door

If your cabinets are the problem, don't take the doors off. That's a permanent change most
leases won't allow, and it looks unfinished if you don't style it constantly. Instead, add a
freestanding open shelf unit next to the cabinets, or a wall-mounted shelf using a no-drill
adhesive mounting system rated for the weight you actually plan to put on it.

Check the weight rating before you load it with your heaviest mixing bowls. I learned that one
the hard way, and it wasn't quiet.

## What I'd skip

Contact paper on counters looks fine in photos and terrible within a week of actual cooking. And
full cabinet refacing kits, even the "removable" ones, tend to leave residue that costs you part
of your deposit. If a product claims to be removable, test it somewhere invisible first, like
inside a cabinet door, before committing to the whole kitchen.

A rental kitchen doesn't have to look like a rental kitchen. It just has to survive the lease and
come apart clean when you go.
