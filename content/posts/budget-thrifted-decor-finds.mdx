Thrift stores get romanticized a lot in decor content. Someone finds a $4 lamp that turns out to
be worth $400, and suddenly everyone thinks every thrift trip ends that way. It doesn't. Most
trips, you leave with nothing, and that's normal.

But when it works, thrifting is genuinely the best way to add character to a room without
spending real money. You just need to know what's worth digging for and what's a trap.

## Buy: solid wood furniture, even if it's ugly

Ugly finish, great bones. That's the entire thesis of furniture thrifting. A side table with a
dated stain but a dovetail joint and real wood construction is worth $15 and an afternoon with
sandpaper. Particleboard furniture with a nice finish is not worth carrying to your car.

Flip it over. Check the joints. If it's solid wood or real veneer over wood, and the structure is
sturdy when you shake it gently, it's probably a yes.

## Buy: brass, glass, and ceramic in odd shapes

Mass-produced decor from big box stores tends to look the same because it is the same, made from
the same few molds sold everywhere. Thrifted brass candlesticks, an oddly shaped ceramic vase, or
a cut glass dish from decades ago has a shape you won't find on a Target endcap.

These are also usually cheap, often under five dollars, because most shoppers walk right past
them. That's your advantage.

## Buy: frames, regardless of what's in them

An empty frame in good condition costs more new than a full frame with someone's 1987 family
photo costs used. Buy the frame. Toss or donate what's inside. Repaint it if the finish isn't
right, or leave it if the patina works.

## Skip: upholstered furniture unless you can see the frame

A stained or worn fabric sofa might be a great frame under bad upholstery, or it might be
structurally shot with a fabric problem on top. Unless you're planning to reupholster it, or
you can confirm the frame and cushions are in real shape, this is a pass. Bed bugs and mystery
stains are not worth the discount.

## Skip: anything electrical you can't test in-store

Lamps, especially older ones, sometimes have wiring that's genuinely a fire risk. If the store
won't let you plug it in to test it, and you're not planning to rewire it yourself, walk past it.
The base can be beautiful. The cord underneath it might not be safe.

## Skip: particleboard "vintage" pieces from the 90s and 2000s

Not everything old is well made. A lot of furniture from the 90s and early 2000s used the same
particleboard construction as fast furniture today, just with an older finish. Check the
material, not the decade.

<Callout>
The best thrift finds usually don't look like much on the shelf. A weird lamp base, a plain
brass tray, a frame with the wrong photo in it. The good eye is for potential, not polish.
</Callout>

## How to actually find the good stuff

Go on weekday mornings if you can, right after new stock hits the floor, before the resellers get
there first. Check the furniture section before the decor shelves, since furniture turns over
slower and gets picked over less. And bring a small tape measure. A "great find" that doesn't fit
your space isn't a find. It's just clutter you paid for.
